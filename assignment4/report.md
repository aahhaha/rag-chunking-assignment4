## 1 Research Question Aim Objectives and Hypothesis
The research question is: How much do different chunking methods (fixed-size, fixed-size with overlap, and semantic chunking) change context quality and answer accuracy in RAG systems? The aim is to compare these methods in the same retrieval and generation pipeline. The objectives remain to build the system with open-source tools and Wikipedia documents, implement the three splitters, measure retrieval with Recall@k, score answers against references, and compare the methods. H1 states that semantic chunking gives better context and more correct answers than fixed-size chunking. The original decision rule requires higher Recall@5 and Exact Match after Holm correction at the 0.05 level.

## 3 Final Experimental Setup
The dataset is SQuAD v1.1 development data (Rajpurkar et al., 2016), containing 10,570 questions across 48 Wikipedia articles. Paragraphs are joined within each article, and retrieval searches the complete article collection. A seeded shuffle selects 500 questions using seed 42. The budget check uses the first 300 questions of the same sample; it is not an independent test set. All questions have reference answers. Article counts and dataset and result-file SHA-256 hashes are stored in analysis.json. All 500 questions were kept in the analysis.

The simple baseline splits articles into 500-character chunks without overlap. The stronger comparison uses the same chunk size and 50-character overlap. The semantic condition uses LangChain SemanticChunker with a 95th-percentile breakpoint threshold. All conditions use normalised all-MiniLM-L6-v2 embeddings, an exact FAISS flat index, five retrieved chunks, and FLAN-T5-base generation from the first three chunks. Generation is greedy, limited to 32 new tokens, with a 512-token input limit. The prompt places context before the question, which matters when truncation removes the end of the input.

A retrieval hit requires an answer string in a chunk from the correct article after SQuAD-style normalisation. Recall@k is the average binary hit indicator, rather than a count of all relevant passages. EM and token F1 take the best score across reference answers. Recall measures whether an answer string was retrieved. EM and F1 measure how closely the generated answer matches a reference. Documents, questions, preprocessing, retrieval depth, prompt, and models are shared across conditions. The splitters produce different chunk lengths, so the same input limit can leave the models with very different amounts of usable evidence. Historical hardware and elapsed time were not preserved in the result CSVs and cannot be reconstructed from the current machine.

## 4 Main Results
Overlap achieved the highest Recall@5, 81.8%, compared with 75.2% for fixed-size and 66.4% for semantic chunking. Its improvement over fixed-size was 6.6 percentage points, or 33 additional successful questions out of 500. Semantic retrieval was 8.8 points below fixed-size and 15.4 points below overlap. Recall@1 and Recall@3 followed the same ordering. Table 2 includes the confidence intervals and generation outcomes; Figure 1 shows the relative size of the retrieval and answer scores.

For Recall@5, overlap succeeded where fixed-size failed on 58 questions, while fixed-size succeeded alone on 25. The imbalance survives Holm correction. In contrast, overlap's EM of 58.0% exceeded fixed-size's 54.8% by 3.2 points, but the paired test did not establish a difference at 0.05. This does not demonstrate equivalence. Semantic EM was 1.4%, and its F1 was approximately 4.0%. Its prompts were truncated on 478 of 500 questions, compared with one fixed-size prompt and no overlap prompts. ## 6 Answering the Research Question and Hypothesis
H1 is not supported in the tested configuration. Semantic chunking is lower than fixed-size on both required outcomes, so it fails the original joint decision rule. This finding concerns the specific percentile splitter, embedding model, generator, and context construction. It does not reject every possible semantic method.

Different chunking methods changed both retrieval quality and answer accuracy substantially in this RAG system. Adding 50-character overlap to 500-character chunks improved Recall@5 from 75.2% to 81.8%, while the EM increase from 54.8% to 58.0% remained statistically uncertain. Semantic chunking returned longer passages but achieved only 66.4% Recall@5. Its 1.4% main-run EM was strongly affected by prompt truncation. The matched budget check and embedding-window groups show that input length is an important explanation to investigate, rather than evidence that semantic coherence is inherently harmful. For this small-model pipeline, overlap provided the best observed retrieval, with more modest evidence of improved final answers.

## 7 Discussion
The clearest finding is that a small overlap improved retrieval without requiring a different embedding model or generator. An answer near a fixed-size boundary can be fragmented across adjacent chunks. Repeating a short boundary region can preserve that answer in a searchable unit, while also changing the embeddings and rankings of neighbouring chunks. However, this report does not equate all 33 additional hits with boundary repair. Without saved ranked chunks, the results alone cannot distinguish a repaired boundary from a ranking change. That distinction would require rebuilding and recording the exact retrieval trace.

The answer scores show why retrieval and generation need separate evaluation. The system retrieves five chunks for measurement but supplies three for generation. There are 27 fixed-size questions and 32 overlap questions with a hit at five but not at three. Even when the answer text appears in the first three, generation can select a nearby wrong entity, produce an incomplete span, or combine fragments. A larger Recall@5 therefore creates an opportunity for better answers; it does not guarantee that the generator receives and uses the needed evidence. Reporting only the highest retrieval score would conceal this limitation.

The semantic result is consistent with the caution in Qu, Tu, and Bao (2025), who evaluated semantic and fixed-size chunking on document retrieval, evidence retrieval, and retrieval-based answer generation. They found that additional computation did not yield consistent improvements. The present study adds a concrete small-window failure case, but it does not reproduce their complete benchmark or measure their cost comparison. Because elapsed time was not logged here, the findings cannot support a numerical claim about whether semantic chunking is cheaper or more expensive overall.

Kiss, Nagy, and Szilágyi (2025) provide a useful counterpoint. Their Max-Min method groups consecutive sentences using semantic similarity and was evaluated through clustering tasks and RAG multiple-choice answering. Their positive results do not directly conflict with this negative result: Max-Min is a different algorithm from the percentile-breakpoint implementation tested here, and the tasks and generation settings differ. A conclusion that all semantic methods fail would therefore exceed the evidence. The splitter has to be evaluated with the models that will actually use its chunks.

Gao et al. (2023, revised 2024) discuss retrieval, augmentation, and generation as connected parts of a RAG pipeline. This study supports that system-level perspective: changing document segmentation changes which text is represented by embeddings and which text can reach the generator. Their paper is a survey, so it supplies an architectural explanation rather than a directly comparable experimental effect. These three sources were identified in the earlier shared literature discussion; the final Assignment 1 bibliography should be checked for exact correspondence before submission.

The unexpected gap between semantic retrieval and generation is especially important. A chunk may contain the gold answer somewhere in its full text while its embedding represents only its beginning. Later, a concatenated prompt can exceed the generator's input limit, potentially removing the question at the end. The saved results establish frequent truncation, and the visibility groups show an association with retrieval failure. They do not identify the exact lost token span in every case. A controlled follow-up would cap chunks by tokens before indexing, preserve the question during prompt construction, and log the final input actually received by the generator.

For someone building a similar system, the result gives a useful starting point. For an English encyclopedic QA system using these compact models, fixed-size overlap is a reasonable configuration to carry into a fresh evaluation. It offers the strongest observed retrieval with passages that fit the embedding window. Semantic splitting should be evaluated together with token limits and context packing, rather than selected solely because its boundaries are intended to preserve meaning. The recommendation applies to this setup; cost and other datasets still need to be tested.

The budget comparison is clearer when it uses the same questions in both runs. The semantic improvement is 23.33 percentage points on the same 300 questions, with a paired bootstrap interval from 18.33 to 28.33 points. This is a within-sample change following a known input-construction problem. It is useful diagnostic evidence, but it does not validate a new configuration on unseen data. Fixed-size EM falls by 1.00 point on those matched questions, while overlap EM is unchanged. The unequal response to the same character cap is consistent with differences in chunk length, although it does not identify which individual supporting sentences were removed.

The available measurements do not establish which method is fastest or cheapest. Semantic retrieval returned about 14,532 characters in the top five, compared with about 2,479 for fixed-size. This describes retrieved text volume, not latency, memory use, or monetary cost. A smaller number of semantic index entries could reduce some storage and search work while longer passages increase other work. Without measurements of both stages, neither index size nor retrieved characters alone establishes the most efficient system.

Internal validity is limited by unequal effective input lengths and the exploratory analyses chosen after seeing results. External validity is limited by one dataset, one language, and a single embedder and generator. Construct validity is limited because normalised answer-string containment may count an irrelevant occurrence as evidence, while EM can penalise acceptable wording. Statistical conclusion validity is limited by related questions from the same articles: question-level resampling assumes more independence than may be justified. Article-cluster resampling would be a useful robustness check on a fresh protocol. Model training contamination is also possible, so absolute QA scores should not be interpreted as pure retrieval-grounded reasoning.

Future work should use a newly frozen protocol and untouched evaluation questions, compare semantic splitting with and without token caps, and align the retrieval and generation budgets. It should retain chunk IDs, ranking scores, tokenised prompts, model revisions, hardware, and runtime logs. Independent verification of the error labels and a clean-environment rerun would strengthen reproducibility. They would help test whether the semantic result changes once the input-window problem is addressed.
